# The Strategy Deck — 34 cards

Thirty-four cards for executives choosing where to invest in AI, distilled from
the AI-Driven Business Innovation masterclass. Six sections: **Frame** (5),
**Foundations** (6), **Balance** (4), **Invest** (9), **Adopt** (5),
**Decide** (5).

One card type: why it goes wrong, the move, the line.

**Fund what it multiplies, not just what it replaces.**

---

## FRAME · Classify the portfolio

### 01 · The transformation matrix
**Why it goes wrong:** Every AI initiative arrives labelled "transformation", so the portfolio has no shape and every pitch sounds the same.

**The move:** Two axes: focus (process or strategic) and magnitude (incremental or transformational). Four quadrants: Optimize, Enhance, Revolutionize, Transform. Place every initiative before you fund any.

*"Place it before you fund it."*

### 02 · The Optimize trap
**Why it goes wrong:** Cost-saving automation is easy to approve and easy to measure, so the whole portfolio quietly clusters in one quadrant.

**The move:** Check the matrix for gaps. Most organisations cluster in Optimize. Strategic leaders balance across all four.

*"An all-Optimize portfolio is a cost program, not a strategy."*

### 03 · Enhance is the quiet middle
**Why it goes wrong:** Enhancement — better products, personalised service — sits between the measurable and the visionary, and gets skipped in both directions.

**The move:** Name at least one Enhance initiative deliberately. Recommendations, personalised service, AI-assisted delivery: they compound with what you already sell.

*"Improve what you sell, not just how you run."*

### 04 · Revolutionize needs more than a pilot budget
**Why it goes wrong:** Reinventing a core process gets scoped like a software project — and dies at pilot scale.

**The move:** If a quadrant-3 play (process, transformational) is on the table, fund it as a program with executive sponsorship, not as a pilot with a pilot's budget.

*"Reinvention at pilot budget is theatre."*

### 05 · The multiplier test
**Why it goes wrong:** AI gets costed as a replacement for labour hours, and the case collapses at the first budget review.

**The move:** Ask what AI multiplies: data, reach, judgement, throughput. Fund what it multiplies, not just what it replaces.

*"AI scales whatever is fed into it, including the flaws."*

## FOUNDATIONS · The data under the ambitions

### 06 · The data value pyramid
**Why it goes wrong:** Ambitions get set at the top of the pyramid while the data sits at the bottom, and no one wrote down the distance.

**The move:** Five levels: collection, integration, descriptive, predictive, prescriptive. Locate the organisation honestly, then locate each ambition.

*"Locate yourself before you schedule the future."*

### 07 · You cannot skip levels
**Why it goes wrong:** Leadership sees prescriptive demos and asks for Level 5 outcomes from Level 2 data.

**The move:** Map every ambition to a level, and fund the levels underneath it. Predictive needs descriptive; prescriptive needs predictive.

*"You cannot skip levels."*

### 08 · Integration before intelligence
**Why it goes wrong:** Point solutions get bought while the data sits in disconnected systems, and every pilot starts by rebuilding the same joins.

**The move:** Customer 360, unified inventory, one view of operations: fund integration as an AI initiative in its own right.

*"Connected data is the first AI product."*

### 09 · Descriptive is not done
**Why it goes wrong:** "We have dashboards" gets counted as analytics maturity, and the gap to predictive stays invisible.

**The move:** Dashboards describe; they don't predict. The move from "what happened" to "what will happen" is a modelling discipline, not a reporting upgrade.

*"'What happened' is a report. 'What will happen' is a different investment."*

### 10 · The honest level score
**Why it goes wrong:** Data readiness gets scored by the team asking for the budget.

**The move:** Score data readiness 0–10 per initiative, with evidence. Below 7: stop, and fund data infrastructure first.

*"Under 7 is a data project wearing an AI costume."*

### 11 · Garbage in, multiplied
**Why it goes wrong:** Data quality problems look survivable at reporting scale and become existential at decision scale.

**The move:** Fix the quality of whatever feeds the model before the model feeds the decisions.

*"AI scales whatever is fed into it, including the flaws."*

## BALANCE · The portfolio across time

### 12 · Three horizons
**Why it goes wrong:** The portfolio gets filled with this year's payback projects, and the 3-to-5-year capabilities never get funded.

**The move:** H1: optimise the core — 70%. H2: build emerging business — 20%. H3: create the future — 10%. Adjust for industry, but hold all three.

*"All H1 is no future. All H3 is no present."*

### 13 · The over-rotation warnings
**Why it goes wrong:** The allocation drifts every budget cycle, and nobody notices until it's lopsided.

**The move:** Check the drift annually: too much H1 means short-term thinking; too much H3 means burning cash on uncertain futures.

*"Watch the drift, not just the split."*

### 14 · H1 pays for H3
**Why it goes wrong:** Horizons get funded as competitors rather than as a system.

**The move:** The measurable wins fund the emerging bets. Say it out loud when H1 business cases are approved: part of the return is H3 budget.

*"The core funds the future."*

### 15 · H3 is an option, not a line item
**Why it goes wrong:** Transformational bets get judged by H1 ROI maths and killed by the wrong test.

**The move:** Value H3 as option value: the right to move fast if the technology or the market turns. Small, patient, watched.

*"H3 buys the right to move fast later."*

## INVEST · The case, the thresholds, the red flags

### 16 · The four value categories
**Why it goes wrong:** Business cases count only cost reduction, so every initiative competes on the same narrow axis.

**The move:** Total value = cost reduction + revenue growth + risk reduction + strategic positioning. Price all four, or the case is wrong before the maths starts.

*"Four categories, or the case is wrong before the maths."*

### 17 · The ROI thresholds
**Why it goes wrong:** One ROI bar gets applied across horizons, so H3 bets die on an H1 test.

**The move:** H1: above 200% in year one. H2: above 150% over three years. H3: above 100% over five years — or a strategic must-have. Low confidence in value raises the bar; high certainty lowers it.

*"Match the threshold to the horizon."*

### 18 · The five-year cost
**Why it goes wrong:** Year-one cost gets approved and the ongoing bill — model upkeep, retraining, monitoring — arrives as a surprise.

**The move:** Continuous learning and upkeep run 30–50% of build cost annually. Calculate five-year TCO, not year-one cost.

*"Calculate the five-year cost, not the year-one cost."*

### 19 · Accuracy against risk
**Why it goes wrong:** A 90% accurate system sounds excellent until you ask what the other 10% touches.

**The move:** Set the accuracy bar by consequence: low stakes 70–80% can be fine; regulated or high-consequence work needs 95–99% and the oversight to match.

*"The bar is set by consequence, not by the demo."*

### 20 · Explainability costs
**Why it goes wrong:** Regulated decisions get built on models nobody can explain, and the gap appears at audit.

**The move:** If the decision is regulated or client-facing, budget 15–20% more for explainability — before the build, not during the audit.

*"Budget the explanation with the build."*

### 21 · The two red flags
**Why it goes wrong:** Scores look clean while the two failures that actually kill projects sit outside the scoring.

**The move:** Automatic override for: automating a human touchpoint with no theory of why it exists, and no validated problem owner. Either flag stops the funding, whatever the ROI.

*"A polished demo is not evidence that anyone needs it."*

### 22 · Highest ROI is not the best investment
**Why it goes wrong:** The ranking by ROI gets treated as the decision, and the projects with AI-specific red flags win on a technicality.

**The move:** Score traditional criteria and AI-specific criteria together — data readiness, learning cost, accuracy against risk, explainability, ethics. Then let the red flags veto.

*"Highest ROI ≠ best AI investment."*

### 23 · One biased system, millions of biased decisions
**Why it goes wrong:** Ethics gets assessed per decision instead of per system.

**The move:** Before funding, ask what the system decides at scale, who it treats differently, and who is accountable when it does. Price the ethics work into the case.

*"Unethical AI is worse than no AI."*

## ADOPT · The journey the organisation can actually walk

### 24 · The five phases
**Why it goes wrong:** Adoption gets planned as one big rollout, skipping the phases where the learning happens.

**The move:** Knowledge building, pilot experimentation, selective deployment, scaling operations, comprehensive transformation. Know the phase you are in before you fund the next.

*"Name the phase before you fund the phase."*

### 25 · Most fail by skipping
**Why it goes wrong:** The visible successes are at Phase 4, so everyone plans for Phase 4.

**The move:** Most organisations fail by trying to skip to scaling. Respect the journey; fund the phase you are in.

*"Most fail by skipping to Phase 4."*

### 26 · The budget shape changes
**Why it goes wrong:** Pilots get tiny budgets, so pilots stay tiny; scaling gets huge budgets, so scaling starts before the pilots taught anything.

**The move:** Budget by phase: 1–5% knowledge, 10–20% pilots, 30–40% selective deployment, 40–50% scaling, then sustained. The shape is the plan.

*"The budget curve is the adoption plan."*

### 27 · Pilots are for learning, not for proving
**Why it goes wrong:** Pilots get run as mini-rollouts to prove the decision was right.

**The move:** A pilot's deliverable is what was learned: where it worked, where it broke, what the scale would need. Write that down, or the pilot was a demo.

*"A pilot that only proves is a demo."*

### 28 · The centre of excellence arrives at scaling
**Why it goes wrong:** The CoE gets stood up first, becomes a bottleneck, and owns standards for work it has never run.

**The move:** Build the CoE from people who ran successful pilots, at the scaling phase — not before.

*"The CoE is built from pilots, not from PowerPoint."*

## DECIDE · The discipline at the decision point

### 29 · Criteria before pitches
**Why it goes wrong:** The weighting gets chosen after the pitches, by people who have already favourites.

**The move:** Agree the weighting criteria before the first pitch, and give the AI-specific criteria real weight — half or more. Then score every pitch against the same sheet.

*"Criteria after pitches are verdicts, not criteria."*

### 30 · The designed dilemma
**Why it goes wrong:** The investment meeting becomes an ROI beauty contest, and the highest-ROI project with the red flag wins by default.

**The move:** Construct the agenda so the highest-ROI option carries a real AI-specific flag — data prep, ethics, budget. If the dilemma doesn't exist, create it: that decision is the practice.

*"Traditional criteria would fund the wrong projects."*

### 31 · Scale, pivot, kill
**Why it goes wrong:** Pilots drift into production or die quietly; both outcomes skip the decision.

**The move:** At the gate: scale on ROI trajectory, feasibility, market readiness, fit and resources; pivot if close but the conditions changed; kill if it missed. Killing is a decision, made on purpose.

*"Scale/Pivot/Kill is a framework, not a formula."*

### 32 · Fast-follow is a strategy
**Why it goes wrong:** Every competitive AI threat triggers a pioneer response, at pioneer cost and pioneer risk.

**The move:** Match the response to the horizon: pioneer (H3), fast-follow (H2), wait and watch (H1). Fast-follow with a funded pathway is often the rational answer.

*"You don't have to be first. You have to be ready."*

### 33 · The five questions
**Why it goes wrong:** Approvals happen on slides, and the five questions that matter never get asked out loud.

**The move:** Before any approval: what problem, whose problem, what data, what accuracy against what risk, who owns the outcome. If the pitch can't answer all five, it isn't ready.

*"If they can't answer all five, you're not ready to approve."*

### 34 · Frameworks, consistently
**Why it goes wrong:** The frameworks get applied to the projects that needed them least, and skipped for the ones that needed them most.

**The move:** Tools, not rules — adapt to context, but use them consistently, especially when the pitch is exciting.

*"Frameworks prevent reactive, emotional decisions."*

---

**Fund what it multiplies, not just what it replaces.**
